## Abstract {-}

The insurer has given the denial to a machine, and the machine answers in seconds. The physician has been given nothing, and must meet in hours what was done in an instant, so most denials are never met at all. The reply owed is a writ in the oldest sense, an instrument issued under a signature that binds its author to every word; a machine may draft it, but a draft that asserts one thing the record does not hold, over a physician's name, is worse than none. I asked whether Meta's models, one of them open weights, could prepare that instrument from the chart with every sentence held to the record by a deterministic check, decline when the record could not support one, at a cost and speed a practice could use, and whether the same check, pointed the other way, would hold the insurer's own determination to the same record. Fifty synthetic claim denials across eighteen high-dollar services, thirty answerable from the chart and twenty not, $940,000 of billed care, were generated with a grading key. Three Meta models drafted responses with the chart in the prompt, by tool calls, and with a live query of a coverage index; a gate reimplemented from the LITMUS papers verified every quote, number, date, code, and side against the cited entry, and 12,242 injected fabrications measured its sensitivity. Post hoc, blocked drafts were sent back once with the gate's reasons, two models ran at higher effort, and the denials were read back from letter images. Across nine kept runs, agreement with the key was 44 to 50 of 50 with no fabricated quote in any sentence; muse-spark-1.3 defended the full $568,500 of the warranted cases in two of its three runs at $0.63 to $0.96 a run; the gate caught 8,602 of 8,602 hard corruptions and none of 2,382 negation or pronoun changes, at 4 to 16 seconds and $0.007 to $0.054 a case; one draft passed on an unsupported case. One repair turn passed six of seven blocked drafts and eroded no decline; higher effort bought muse-spark-1.3 no agreement and the open-weights model 50 of 50 at medium; the letters were read with all eight fields correct in 46 of 50, every error one glyph. Pointed at the plan's determination, the gate refuted the one fabricated quote and passed a denial whose every sentence was in the record and whose reading was wrong: one chart, two verified conclusions, no side taken. The gate verifies that a statement is in the record, not that the record satisfies the criterion; the machine prepares the instrument and cannot issue it, and the physician's signature is what makes the draft a writ.

# Introduction

## The problem

In 2023, Medicare Advantage insurers made 49.8 million prior authorization determinations and denied 3.2 million of them, 6.4 percent. About 11.7 percent of the denials were appealed, and 81.7 percent of those appeals were overturned [1]. Derived from those rates, about 374,000 denials were appealed, about 306,000 were reversed, and about 2.8 million were never challenged at all (Figure 1). When denials are challenged, most fall. Most are never challenged, because the cost of answering lands on the people with the least time: physicians report 13 hours a week spent on prior authorization, and 79 percent say patients have abandoned treatment while waiting [2].

![Medicare Advantage prior authorization, 2023: requests denied, appealed, overturned, and never appealed, derived from the reported rates [1].](figures/attrition.pdf){#fig:attrition width=100%}

The rules are not uniform. Each payer writes its own, and in 2022 the Inspector General of the Department of Health and Human Services found Medicare Advantage organizations applying clinical criteria that Medicare itself does not use, and denying 13 percent of a sample of requests that met Medicare coverage rules [3].

The surgeon whose judgment is overridden spent four years in medical school and five or more in residency and specialty training, and carries the license, the liability, and the duty to see the patient through. The decision to refuse carries none of that weight. I do not think most of the people who build these systems set out to harm patients. But a system rewarded for saying no, and rarely asked to show its reasons, will say no more often than it should.

The insurers dispute these characterizations: UnitedHealth says nH Predict only guides care planning, called the Wall Street Journal's analysis of its diagnoses inaccurate and biased, and says it has full confidence in its practices; Cigna says PxDx is not artificial intelligence.

## A standard, not a product

No machine should make a claim about a patient's care that it cannot support. It must cite its source or show its derivation. If it cannot, it stops, and the physician decides. I wrote that standard down as LITMUS and published it openly under CC BY 4.0, so anyone can use it, test it, or argue with it [11, 12]. It applies to an insurer's algorithm as much as to mine. The position paper argues that an autonomous system can be trusted only in proportion to the strength of the external check that verifies it, that medicine has one valid check, the certified specialist physician, and that the machine may act alone only where consensus is explicit and settled and must defer to the physician everywhere else. The architecture companion specifies the implied system: a model-pluggable agent runtime, an extensible registry of tools, and a fail-closed verification spine whose criteria are curated per specialty, with every claim, gate decision, and verdict recorded in a provenance layer.

## What is not known

Large language models can write a medical-necessity letter that reads well. Whether one can write such a letter without stating anything the chart does not contain, under a check that can prove it did not, has not been measured on a set anyone can rerun. Nor has the second half of the task been measured: whether the model declines when the record cannot answer the denial, including the administrative denials for which a medical-necessity appeal is the wrong instrument. The LITMUS papers predict the shape of the answer, a deterministic check that verifies presence in the record and not sufficiency against the criterion, but they contain no measurement.

## Objective and hypothesis

The objective was to measure, on a fixed synthetic set with a grading key, whether three Meta models, one of them open weights, can draft the physician's response to a claim denial from the chart with every sentence verified against the record, decline when the record cannot answer, and do so at a cost and latency a practice could use. The hypothesis: a deterministic referential gate reduces fabricated statements in model-drafted appeals to zero on this set, at the cost of some true derivations blocked, without reducing agreement with the key below ninety percent. The secondary question was whether a model given the chart as an index and two tools, rather than whole, reads what a reviewer would read.

## The system under test

A denial arrives with its reason code and the payer's remark (Figure 2). The model is given the payer's written criteria, the denial, the case facts, and the chart, and asked for one of two things: a decision that the record does not support an appeal, with the reason, or a draft in which every sentence carries one citation to one chart entry and a verbatim quote from it. The gate then checks every sentence. If the record does not support an appeal, the software says so instead of arguing anyway. Nothing is sent without a physician's signature. The viewer renders a passing draft as that instrument: the payer's reason quoted at the head, each sentence footnoted to the entry, date, and verbatim quote the gate verified it against, the gate version and the input hash at the foot, and a blank signature line under a band that reads draft, unsigned, not sent. It can be printed or saved as text; the page sends nothing. The software gives no advice to patients, recommends no treatment, and sends nothing on its own.

## The fifty cases

**What kind of denial.** Every case is a claim denied after the service was given: the payer's remittance, carrying a claim adjustment reason code (CARC) and the payer's remark, for a procedure, admission, drug, or continued stay already delivered. None is a prior-authorization request refused before care. The statistics in section 1.1 are prior-authorization figures because that is where the public record is richest; the response to either kind of denial is the same document, the physician's record read against the payer's written criteria, and the set should be read as a test of that document, not of the prior-authorization process. Thirty-four cases carry CARC 50, not medically necessary. Three carry CARC 197, no authorization on file: in two the plan allows a retrospective medical-necessity review and the chart answers it, and in one the plan refuses that review, so a medical-necessity appeal is the wrong instrument and the right answer is to decline. The rest are experimental or investigational (CARC 56), non-covered (96, 167), diagnosis or modifier inconsistent with the procedure (11, 4), frequency (151), benefit maximum (119), and timely filing (29).

**How they were made.** The set is generated, not hand-written. A generator builds all fifty deterministically from a fixed seed (20261027) using 18 service templates, one per service, and a test checks that the committed files match the generator, so anyone can regenerate them and get the same bytes. A fixed plan allocates 30 warranted cases, where the chart documents every criterion, and 20 that are not, in four kinds: 6 where the chart never addresses a criterion, 7 where it raises the criterion but falls short, 4 administrative denials no chart can answer, and 3 plan exclusions. One case, case-023, was relabeled from the first kind to the second on October 30, after the archived version, with the reason in section 4.6; its text is unchanged. Each template writes the chart in dictation voice, the way a clinician speaks a note rather than the way a payer checklist reads, and the generator adds the routine traffic a real chart has, a referral letter, a blood-pressure check, a pharmacy call, so that about two thirds of the 19 to 35 entries (mean 25.6) are unrelated and a citation has to land on the right one. The six payers are fictional, alternating a standard and a strict criteria profile, and the criteria attributed to them were written for this benchmark from publicly described policy patterns; they are not any insurer's policy. A patient is an alias, an age, and a sex. Each case carries its grading key: whether an appeal is warranted, which criteria the chart meets, which entries support each, which entries are unrelated, and a one-line rationale. The key was written by the same person who wrote the templates, and four of its labels were wrong until the models found them (section 4.4).

Read as an experiment, the thirty warranted cases are positive controls, the twenty others are negative controls of four kinds, the unrelated entries in every chart are the noise floor for citation precision, and the injected fabrications of section 2.7 are spike-ins.

## The gate

The gate was reimplemented during the event from the published papers and not copied from any prior system. It is deterministic and sits outside the model. For each sentence it resolves the citation to one chart entry; requires the quote to appear verbatim in that entry, with whitespace, case, quote glyphs, dash variants, and compatibility forms such as fullwidth digits normalized and nothing else, and at least one word of it not a stopword; and requires every hard token in the sentence to appear in the cited entry, the payer criteria, the denial, or the stated case facts. A hard token is a number (written in digits, in words, as an ordinal, as the article a or an before a unit, or as a Roman numeral after a grading word), a date (in any written form, held as one), a code (letter, system word, and digits), or a side (left, right, or an abbreviation of either). The day and the month of a date are not admissible as bare numbers. From v1.7 a number set beside a side keeps that side: where the sources pair the number with the other side, the sentence is refuted, so "690 on the right" is not sourced by an entry that puts 690 on the left. A sentence that fails is refuted with the token named, or, from v1.3, returned as needs-citation when every missing token lives in some other entry of the same chart. A check that throws is an error, never a pass. A draft passes only when every sentence verifies and every criterion the draft claims has verified evidence; otherwise it is blocked. The gate has no resemblance threshold and no keyword list. Its version is stamped into every verdict, and because it is a pure function of its inputs, any stored draft can be re-gated under a later version and the change recorded.

The versions after v1 each added a token class or closed a defect, with a test: compound number words read as one number and hundreds included (v1.1, v1.2); the needs-citation label (v1.3); a side treated as a hard token wherever it names one (v1.4); a side word inside an idiom admitted from no source, in the entry as well as in the sentence (v1.5); the six classes an external review found (v1.6, section 4.4); and the article, ordinal, Roman numeral, and side-number pairing classes (v1.7).

The same gate runs in the viewer. Once a replay has reached its verdict, any sentence can be edited in the page, its words, its quote, or its citation, and re-checked by the gate bundled into the browser from the same module and the same admissible sources; the result is stamped with its reason, nothing is saved, and the stored verdict does not change. It is the spike-in of section 2.7 as a control a reader can turn by hand.

## Models, modes, and the live policy query

Three models were tested: muse-spark-1.3 and muse-wonder-1.0 through the Meta Model API, and Muse Spark Open, the open-weights model, hosted on Baseten, all at effort low with eight cases in flight (three for Baseten after a run at eight lost cases to rate limiting). The three kept Baseten runs of October 27 and 28 used the model id meta-models/muse-spark-open on the workspace key, and every later run the hackathon id meta-models/muse-spark-open-1026; both serve the same model, and each result file records which. Each model ran in three modes. In the first the whole chart is in the prompt. In the second, the agentic mode, the model receives an index, one line per entry with the id, date, kind, and opening words, and two tools, list_entries and read_entry, and reads what it decides it needs; every tool call is kept in a trace. The third adds a tool, policy_status, which queries CHART, the author's pre-existing coverage index of United States payer policy, for when the named authority's coverage material last changed and was last checked. It returns no policy text, so the criteria in the request remain the criteria, and its answer is labeled as a live query against a prior-work data source wherever it appears. The viewer replays any case from any kept run one tool call at a time [15].

## The advisor

After the gate, a second model (muse-wonder-1.0) is shown each criterion with the quotes the draft cited for it and asked one question: do these quotes, read literally, satisfy the criterion? It answers supports, partial, or silent, with one sentence why. The answer is stored with the model's name and shown beside the criterion in the viewer. It never feeds the gate and never changes an outcome; it is an opinion with a name on it, for the physician to weigh.

## Outcome measures and analysis

The primary measure is agreement with the grading key: a case counts as agreed when the model drafts an appeal for a case the key labels warranted and the gate verifies every sentence, or when the model declines a case the key labels unsupported. The other outcomes are blocked (the model drafted and the gate stopped it), missed (a decline on a warranted case), false pass (the gate passed a draft on a case the key labels unsupported), and failed (no valid answer); the five sum to 50. Secondary measures: fabricated quotes (a quote not found verbatim in the cited entry, in a sentence or in an evidence item, on any draft including declined ones), sentences verified, citation precision (the share of cited entries the key names as evidence), entries read per case and the share of the key's evidence entries opened (agentic modes), care defended (the billed value of the warranted cases whose appeal passed, out of $568,500 across the thirty warranted cases and $940,000 across all fifty, reference values of the synthetic set and not a payer's), median latency, and cost per case from the provider's token prices.

**Spike-in.** The gate's sensitivity cannot be read off runs in which no model fabricated anything, so it was measured by spike-in: each sentence the kept runs verified was corrupted one way at a time and re-gated. Corruptions were classed as hard (a number replaced by a value absent from every admissible source, the citation moved to an entry lacking the quote, a word dropped from the quote, a date shifted, a side swapped, and the classes added at v1.6 and v1.7), collision (a wrong number or side that happens to appear in an admissible source), and soft (a negation flipped, a pronoun swapped). Sensitivity is the share caught, where caught means the gate returned refuted or needs-citation; where none was missed, the 95 percent upper bound on the miss rate is given by the rule of three. Agreement counts carry 95 percent Wilson intervals. Run-to-run variance was measured by repeating each live-query configuration with the same cases and settings. Fifty cases is a small set: one case is two percentage points.

**The other chair.** A post hoc run put the gate on the other side of the exchange, added October 29 after the kept runs. The same three models, over the same fifty charts and criteria, were asked for the plan's written claim determination instead of the physician's appeal, under the same contract and the same gate, with the key inverted: a denial is warranted exactly where the record fails a criterion. The outcomes mirror the primary ones: declined to deny where the record supports the service, a verified determination where it does not, a determination written against a record that supports the service (the symmetric false pass), blocked, and failed. These runs are kept apart from Tables 3 and 4 and from the viewer's scoreboard.

## Post hoc additions of October 30

**The repair turn.** The harness took a repair option (default 0; every kept run was at 0). When the gate blocks a warranted draft and a repair remains, the harness sends the model one more user turn in the same exchange: the gate's record, verbatim, one line per finding, each unverified sentence with its index, status, and reason, and, for a needs-citation, the entries the gate names as holding the token, then the draft-level reasons. The model is asked for the complete revised draft under the same schema, keeps its tools and its earlier reads in the agentic mode, and is told that declining is an answer. The revised draft is re-gated under the same gate. A declined draft is never offered a repair: the model's decision to decline stands. The result file keeps the first gate record and the outcome scored on it beside the final outcome, and every repair turn with the reasons sent, the revised draft, its gate record, its tokens, and its latency. The summary reports separately how many repairs turned a blocked draft on an unsupported case into a pass, which would be the repair eroding the decline. The three live-query configurations were run again with one repair.

**The stated reason.** The Meta Model API returns the model's reasoning only as redacted thinking blocks, so there is no thought text to show. Instead, the two tools that open something, read_entry and policy_status, took a required field, why: one sentence on what the model expects the entry to bear on and why it is opening it now. The executor ignores the field; the trace records it, and the viewer shows it under the chart column as the entry opens. The repair runs are the first to carry it.

**The effort sweep.** muse-spark-1.3 and Muse Spark Open were run again in the live-query configuration at effort medium and at effort high: the same 50 cases, the same tools, the same 12,288-token output cap, the same gate. Effort is the API's output_config.effort, set explicitly on every call; on the Meta Model API the reasoning it buys is reported as thinking tokens and billed as output, and on the Baseten endpoint it is not reported. muse-wonder-1.0 was not swept: its kept run agreed on 50 of 50 and it is the costliest of the three.

**The denial as a letter, and its intake.** A practice does not receive a case file; it receives a remittance. Each of the fifty denials was rendered as a one-page remittance letter image, 1275 by 1650 pixels, deterministic from the case file: a fictional payer letterhead, the patient alias with age and sex, one claim line with the code and its code system, the dates of service, the billed amount and nothing allowed, the CARC and its text, the remittance advice remark code (RARC) where there is one, the payer's remark, and an appeal-rights paragraph with that payer's appeal window. No member, subscriber, or claim identifier appears, because the set issues none, and the page says so. Each image was sent to muse-spark-1.3 as an image block with a schema for eight fields (the CARC, the remark, the first and last dates of service, the code, the code system, the billed amount, and the payer's name), and each field read was scored against the case file: exact after trimming for codes and dates, exact after whitespace and quote normalization for the remark and the payer's name, a number for the amount, empty for no service end. This is document intake of an administrative letter and nothing clinical: no chart entry is sent, no clinical judgment is asked for, and the model is told to transcribe what is printed and to leave a field empty rather than guess.

**Dictation.** The chart entry a draft quotes is, in practice, dictated. A command transcribes a recording with Muse Voice Transcribe through the Meta Model API, writes the transcript as that entry's text into a copy of the case with the original kept beside it, and runs the primary configuration on that one case, so that every quote the gate verifies against the entry is a quote of speech, and a transcription error becomes a fact of the record the gate will hold the draft to. The pipeline was proven end to end on a test clip of the same entry read by a text-to-speech voice (28.8 seconds; not the author's voice, and not committed); the author's own recording is not yet made, and the viewer does not show the stand-in.

## Reproducibility

The test suite holds 282 tests. The fifty cases regenerate byte for byte from the seed. A harness runs a model over the set and writes a result file whose header records the model, mode, gate version, and any later re-gate. The spike-in reruns from the stored result files. The build log and the tables of section 3 regenerate from the result files; nothing in those tables is typed by hand. The gate ships on its own as a command-line tool and a Model Context Protocol server, published on npm as litmus-gate [16], so a drafting agent on any host can be held to the record before its draft is shown to anyone; a test checks that the package reproduces every stored verdict and hash of a kept run. The viewer replays any case from any kept run, lays the three models' results for one case side by side, and is checked in headless Chromium. The repository is deposited at Zenodo with a DOI for each version [14].

# Discussion

## Interpretation

The hypothesis held on this set. No model fabricated a quote in a sentence in seventeen runs; the two fabricated in evidence items were on drafts the model declined, and the gate refuted them. The spike-in shows why the absence is informative: every hard corruption injected into a verified sentence was caught, so a fabricated number, date, code, side, quote, or citation in a passing draft would have been found, unless the wrong value happened to appear in an admissible source (section 4.3). The cost of that guarantee is visible in the blocked column: nine drafts stopped for sentences that were true to the chart but cited one entry for a fact that lives in another, or stated a count the chart never states, and two empty warranted answers stopped for having nothing to verify. The design favors blocking a true derivation over passing an invented one, and the physician is told what to fix. Agreement stayed at or above 90 percent in eight of the nine kept runs; the ninth, Muse Spark Open with the live query, stands at 88 percent after v1.7 blocked a fifth true sentence, so the hypothesis held in eight runs and missed by one case in the ninth, and every one of that run's six disagreements is a blocked true sentence or the one false pass, not a fabrication.

The secondary question also answered itself. Given an index and two tools, the models read the chart the way a reviewer does: a third of the entries, almost all of the ones that matter, nothing at all for a denial the chart cannot answer, and nearly everything before declining a case where the evidence is absent.

## The disagreements, read

muse-wonder-1.0 declined a wound-care case the key calls warranted. The criterion lists infection control and glycemic management among the components of standard wound care. The weekly notes document debridement, dressings, and offloading, and the chart holds a normal hemoglobin A1c and a clean wound elsewhere. The model read the criterion strictly and said the record did not meet it. That is a judgment a physician could share. It is not an invented fact.

The gate blocked a true count: "three antianginal agents," citing a note that lists metoprolol, amlodipine, and isosorbide. The count is correct and the gate blocked it, because the number three appears nowhere in the record. The gate cannot count. Whether to exempt simple counts, with the derivation recorded, is an open question.

Twice in the kept runs and three times in one repeat, a model set warranted to true on an absent case with no evidence and no sentences. The gate blocked every one. That is the fail-closed design doing its job, and it is also the clearest sign that declining is the harder half of the task.

More effort did not buy more agreement on this set (Table 9). For muse-spark-1.3 the added reasoning went into not committing: at high effort four answers set warranted to true and wrote nothing, three of them on cases the key labels unsupported, where the same model at low effort had declined with a reason. The gate blocked every one, so nothing false left, but an empty warranted answer is a worse answer than a decline, because it tells the physician nothing. For Muse Spark Open, medium effort gave the best run of any in this study, 50 of 50 with the case-023 draft declined, and high effort cost it two answers to the output cap at twice the latency. Effort low was fixed before the kept runs and stays the reported setting; these four files say that the open-weights model's disagreements at low were not a ceiling of the model, and that one run of fifty at one setting is too few to call medium its right setting.

The repair turn (Table 8) is the answer to the first two of these that costs nothing in the gate. Told which entry holds the number it stated, the model cited it, and six of the seven drafts blocked for a true derivation passed on the second turn at about a twentieth of a run's cost; the gate did not change and still cannot count. The seventh, the empty warranted answer on case-023, came back empty and stayed blocked, which is the behavior the design wants: a repair that argued a case the record does not support would be the repair eroding the decline, and in seven repairs none did. Seven is a small count, and the column that would show the erosion is in the table so a larger run can be read the same way. The stated reason changes nothing in the outcomes and everything in what a reader can see: with no thought text from the API, the one sentence the model writes before each read is the only account of why it opened what it opened, and in the three repair runs it never omitted one.

## The referential limit

Case-023 is the most important result here, and it is on the public scoreboard rather than in a superseded run. The gate checks that every statement is in the record, not that the record satisfies the criterion. "Plan 5 to 6 days a week" answers the frequency half of a criterion and not the intensity half, and the gate passed it because the words are real. The LITMUS papers name this the referential limit: it is a referential warrant, and it verifies consistency, not correctness [11]. The spike-in puts a number on the same limit from the other side: 1,251 of 1,258 wrong values that happened to exist in an admissible source passed, and 2,382 changes with no hard token in them, a flipped negation or a swapped pronoun, passed (Table 6). The advisor caught case-023 and the gate cannot, which is the division of labor the design intends: the gate answers whether a statement is in the record, the advisor offers an opinion on whether the record satisfies the criterion, and the physician who signs is the check on the second question.

The post hoc run from the other chair (Table 7) says the same thing from the insurer's side. The gate held the plan's determination to the same record it holds the physician's appeal to, and it took no side: the one fabricated quote in a sentence in any full run, plan-exclusion language a model invented while arguing the insurer's case, was refuted, and a determination that misread a six-visit program as short of six months passed because its words were in the record. What the gate cannot see is the flag. In the probe, a model set the denial to stand and then wrote six sentences that each support the care; every sentence verified, and only a reader can see that the letter argues against its own conclusion. The standard applies to the insurer's algorithm as much as to this one, and so does its limit.

## What the models found wrong with the test

Four of my grading keys were defective, and each was found by a model reading the chart more carefully than I had. A rehabilitation chart labeled warranted documented two physician visits in 14 days against a criterion of three a week. A sepsis chart labeled absent still carried a lactate and a blood pressure in a later note. A fusion chart labeled absent still mentioned the slip and the motion on the films. A carotid chart put surgery outside the 14-day window the criterion requires. Each was fixed by regenerating only the affected cases, and the ledger records every one. A benchmark written by one person has that person's blind spots, and the models were useful reviewers of it.

The same held for the code. After two cycles of the author's own multi-agent review, Meta's coding agent, Muse Code, reviewed the repository twice from a clone with no network and nothing writable: once on muse-spark-1.3 at effort high as a single agent, once on muse-wonder-1.0 at effort xhigh with the workspace trusted. Both were asked to attack the gate with runnable inputs, and both found token classes the gate never had: a count written as "one" or "a", a date written with a month name, a side written R or L, a code whose letter or system word is wrong, a two-stopword quote that satisfies the verbatim bar, and a number recombined from a date or from a height and weight in the cited entry. None of these moved a stored verdict at the time, and none was among the 9,518 corruptions of the v1.5 spike-in, which is the point: the spike-in measures the corruption shapes its author thought of. The two reports, the prompt, and the complete traces of both runs are in the repository, unedited. The gate findings became six spike-in classes and gate v1.6 the same night, with the reproductions as tests, and the four classes the review left open closed as v1.7 the next evening; section 3 reports what each regate moved.

## In context

The tools in the public record decide in about a second: Cigna's PxDx averaged 1.2 seconds per denial, and UnitedHealth's post-acute denial rate rose from 8.7 to 22.7 percent in four years [5, 6]. The response measured here takes four to sixteen seconds and a few cents, which is the first time the two sides of the exchange have run at comparable speed. The Inspector General's finding that Medicare Advantage plans applied clinical criteria not in Medicare's coverage rules [3] is the reason the design places the payer's criteria in force on the date of service beside the record rather than asking the model what the rules are. And the result that most changed the test, four grading keys corrected by models reading more carefully than their author, suggests a use for these models before any letter is drafted: as reviewers of the reviewer.

A filter circulated in October 2026 holds that an efficiency in United States healthcare nets to zero when both sides can buy the same model [17]. This design is built to pass it. The model is the part both sides can buy. The chart is held by one side only, and a denial is a claim about a policy while an appeal is a claim about the record. And the gate is not a model but a shared standard, published on its own, that lets the other side check what the record says rather than contest whose bot drafted better.

## Limitations

The gate checks consistency, not correctness. It verifies that the quoted words are in the cited entry and that every number, date, code, and side in the sentence is sourced. It does not verify that those words satisfy the criterion. A sentence that carries no hard token is verified by any quote that exists in the entry it cites. Case-023 is the measured instance, and the physician's signature is the design's answer to it.

The gate cannot derive. A count read off a list, a duration computed from two dates, or a side inferred from an entry that names it elsewhere is blocked unless the token appears verbatim. From v1.6 this includes a count written as "one" where the entry says "within the hour", and from v1.7 a count written as "a week" or "a year" where the entry says "this week" or "15 months": eleven true sentences across the runs are blocked for it, four of them in the kept runs.

Token classes remain outside the gate: a count derived from a list, a Roman numeral with no grading word before it, a side-number pair cut across a conjunction or punctuation the clause rule does not recognize, and any frequency or discourse idiom the fixed mask lists do not name; each is a candidate spike-in class.

A sentence is not checked against its own quote. The gate requires the quote to be in the entry and every hard token in the sentence to be sourced somewhere admissible; it does not require the sentence's numbers to agree with the quote beside them. The collision class of the spike-in measures exactly this, and about a fifth of those sentences contradict their own quote and pass. Checking the sentence against the quoted span is the next rule to add, with the derivation cases above as the cost to weigh.

Soft words are not checked. A model once wrote "he" for a female patient and nothing tripped the gate. Sex, age, and alias are case facts and could be checked next; the spike-in gives the size of the blind spot.

One label in the key was corrected after the first deposit. Case-023 was labeled absent, meaning no entry addresses the intensity criterion, yet its screening note says "three disciplines at this intensity" and a progress note "the first full therapy day", lines it shares with its near-miss sibling case-034. By the key's own definitions the chart raises the criterion without meeting it, so on October 30 the label was changed to near-miss in the generator, with the reason in the case's note, and the stored result files were updated to carry it; the chart text and every verdict and hash are unchanged, the archived version carries the old label, and the one false pass still sits on this case.

The counts are small, and the author graded his own test. Fifty synthetic cases, one seed. The charts, the criteria, and the key were written by one person, which makes the test easier than a real chart against a real policy and leaves blind spots the models found four of. Six of the nine kept rows are single runs.

The data are synthetic. No result here says anything about performance on real records, and that measurement cannot be made in public.

The denials are post-service claims. The prior-authorization refusal before care, which the statistics in section 1.1 describe, is the same document answered in a different process, and it was not tested.

Cost and latency are the API's. Muse Spark Open reported no reasoning tokens on the Baseten path, so its cost may be understated; latency is wall-clock from one client and not a practice's network.

## What should come next

A prospective study inside a practice, under its own governance and not in public, with the endpoints that matter: physician minutes per appeal, the share of drafts signed without edit, and the overturn rate against the practice's own history. That practice exists; the author's own practice runs on the pre-existing systems named in the Declarations, so the study waits on governance, not on building. Then prior-authorization cases and the peer-to-peer; sex, age, and alias as checked facts, and a recorded derivation for simple counts; a second seed and an independent author for the key, with inter-rater agreement reported; real policy text from a coverage index in place of synthetic criteria; and the gate and spike-in harness used on other drafting systems, since the standard applies to an insurer's algorithm as much as to this one.

# Conclusion

A physician can now answer a denial at the speed and cost at which it was issued, with every statement held to their own record by a check that has not let a fabrication through in 8,602 tries. The check verifies presence, not sufficiency. The machine prepares the instrument; it cannot issue it. The physician's signature is what makes it a writ.
